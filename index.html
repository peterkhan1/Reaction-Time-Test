What affects your reaction speed?

  • Age: reaction times are usually fastest in young adults and slow gradually over the years.
  • Sleep and focus: tiredness and distraction add noticeable delay.
  • Type of signal: people often react a little faster to sound than to a visual cue.
  • Your device: screen refresh rate (a 60 Hz screen redraws every 16.7 ms), mouse or touch input and system load all add milliseconds.
  • Anticipation: guessing the signal causes false starts, which is why the delay is random.
How it works

How this reaction speed test works

A simple five-round test measured with your browser's high-resolution timer.

The five-round process

  1. Click the test area (or press Space) to begin.
  2. The screen turns red. Wait and do not press anything.
  3. After a random 1.2 to 3 second delay it turns green.
  4. Click, tap or press Space or Enter as fast as you can.
  5. Repeat for five valid rounds. Your results appear in the same box.

How accurate is it?

The timer starts on the frame where the screen turns green and stops on your input, so the result includes your display and input-device delay. That makes it ideal for comparing yourself over time on the same device, but not a laboratory measurement.

Your results are the average, median, best, slowest and a consistency score based on how close your five rounds are. See exactly how the timing works.

Practice

How to improve your reaction time

Small, repeatable habits make the biggest difference to your average.

Before you test

  • Sleep well and test when you are alert.
  • Close heavy tabs and apps so your browser runs smoothly.
  • Use a wired mouse or keyboard and a high refresh rate screen if you have one.

During the test

  • Rest your finger on the button and look at the centre of the screen.
  • Stay relaxed. Do not guess the timing, because early clicks are not counted.
  • Judge yourself on the five-round average, not on a single lucky click.
Research

What the research says

Two large studies are worth knowing.

Woods et al., 2015

Measured simple visual reaction time in 1,469 healthy adults aged 18 to 65, reporting a mean of 231 ms uncorrected and 213 ms after accounting for equipment latency.

MindCrowd (Talboom et al., 2021)

Collected a web-based simple visual reaction task from 75,666 adults aged 18 to 85, showing that browser tests work at large scale and that speed changes across adulthood.

Questions

Reaction time test FAQ

Quick answers about reaction speed, accuracy and how to get a better score.

What is a good reaction time?

For a visual test like this one, anything under about 200 ms is considered good, and under 170 ms is excellent. Most people land between 200 and 300 ms. See the benchmark table above for how this site labels each range.

What is the average human reaction time?

Online tests commonly report an average of roughly 250 ms for a visual signal, though results vary with age, device, display and test design. A large 2015 lab study found a mean of 231 ms. Treat it as a rough reference point, not a fixed standard.

How does this reaction time test work?

Click the test area to begin. The screen turns red, then turns green after a random delay of 1.2 to 3 seconds. Click, tap or press Space or Enter as soon as it turns green. Five valid rounds give you your average, median, best and slowest times.

Why do my results change every time?

Reaction time naturally varies from attempt to attempt because of attention, tiredness and anticipation. Your device matters too: display refresh rate, input lag and background load all add small delays. Compare the average of five rounds rather than a single click.

Do my monitor and mouse affect my reaction time?

Yes, slightly. A 60 Hz display updates about every 16.7 ms, while a 144 Hz display updates about every 6.9 ms. Wired mice and keyboards usually add less delay than wireless or touch input. Use the same setup when comparing results.

How can I improve my reaction time?

Get enough sleep, stay focused and warm up with a few practice rounds. Keep the same device and input method, and look at the centre of the screen rather than scanning. Gains from practice are real but level off after a while.

What happens if I click too early?

That round is not counted. The screen shows "Too soon!" and you click again to repeat the same round, so every result in your average is a genuine reaction to the green signal.

Can I use my keyboard or phone?

Yes. Mouse click, touch, Space and Enter all work. For the fairest comparison, stick with one input method.

Is this a medical test?

No. It is a browser-based benchmark for practice and casual comparison, not a medical or laboratory assessment. Speak to a doctor if you are worried about your reflexes.

Is my data saved or shared?

No account is needed. Your recent sessions are stored only in your own browser, and you can clear them any time from the history section.
