Average reaction time

What is normal, what large studies found, and why a browser test reads a little higher than a lab.

Updated 9 October 2026Sources: Woods 2015, Talboom 2021

213ms after equipment correction

In short

  • Large studies put mean simple visual reaction time in adults at the low 200s of milliseconds.
  • Browser results run higher because display and input delay are included.
  • Your own average of five rounds is the number to track.

01What the research found

Woods et al. (2015) measured simple visual reaction time in 1,469 healthy adults aged 18 to 65 and reported a mean of 231 ms, or 213 ms once equipment latency was accounted for.

The MindCrowd project (Talboom et al., 2021) collected a web-based simple visual reaction task from 75,666 adults aged 18 to 85, showing that speed changes across adulthood and that large web studies are practical.

02Why browser tests read higher

A browser test measures more than your nerves and muscles. The time includes the screen drawing the colour change, the mouse or keyboard reporting the press, and the browser processing it. A 60 Hz screen alone can add up to one 16.7 ms frame.

04What affects your number

  • Age. Reaction time tends to slow gradually through adulthood.
  • Tiredness and attention. Poor sleep and distraction slow and destabilise results.
  • Anticipation. Guessing makes you faster but is not reaction.
  • Hardware. Display refresh rate, mouse polling rate and wireless or touch input all add delay.
  • Signal type. Simple auditory reactions are generally somewhat faster than visual ones. This test uses a visual signal.

05How to compare fairly

  • Use the average of five rounds, not your best click.
  • Use the same device, input method and display every time.
  • Test at a similar time of day, rested.
  • Compare against your own history before comparing with others.
