How the reaction time test works

A plain-English look at the random delay, the timing, the scoring and the limits.

Updated 9 October 2026
On this page
  1. What is being measured
  2. How the timing works
  3. False starts and impossible times
  4. How results are scored
  5. What your hardware adds
  6. Limits

In short

  • Random 1.2 to 3 second delay, instant colour change.
  • Time is stamped on the frame that shows green.
  • Average of five valid rounds; false starts repeat.

01What is being measured

This is a simple reaction time test: one signal, one response. It does not measure choice reactions (picking between options), which take longer.

02How the timing works

  1. You click to begin and the box turns red.
  2. After a random delay of 1.2 to 3 seconds, the box turns green. The delay is random so you cannot predict it.
  3. The timer starts on the browser frame where the green is actually painted, using the high-resolution clock, not when our code asked for it.
  4. Your reaction is the difference between that moment and the timestamp of your click, tap or key press.

The colour change is instant, with no fade, so the signal is not blurred in time.

03False starts and impossible times

Pressing before green is a false start. That round does not count and repeats. A press within 100 ms of green is also treated as a guess, since human visual reactions are not that fast.

04How results are scored

  • Average: the mean of five valid rounds.
  • Median: the middle round, which ignores one lucky or unlucky click.
  • Best and slowest: the fastest and slowest rounds.
  • Consistency: 100 minus the standard deviation divided by the average, as a percentage. Closer to 100 is steadier.

05What your hardware adds

06Limits

No browser test can remove device latency. Use it to compare yourself over time on the same setup. It is not a medical or laboratory measurement.
